# The RFP document ends at the proposal due date; the process after it produces records and a contract, and returns upstream only as a changed requirement

## In short

The sources agree on a clean seam, and it lies at the proposal due
date, not at the moment of issue. Before that date the RFP is a
document that changes by amendment: vendor questions are answered to
all bidders, and a change to specification, schedule or deadline is
issued as an amendment that "becomes an integral part of the RFP"
[2]. After that date nothing is written into the RFP any more: what
follows (clarification of what was submitted, discussions,
negotiation with the leading bidder, a final offer, award) is run
under equal-treatment rules, leaves an audit trail and ends in a
contract into which the RFP, its amendments and the proposal are
folded as exhibits ranked below the agreement [6][7][8][14]. The
document itself carries the rules of the process it will be run
under (how to ask, how proposals are evaluated, the deadlines), so
"document" is not the same as "requirements" [4][9]. What returns
upstream are two things only: a vendor question that shows a
requirement unclear (answered by amendment, before the due date) and
an evaluation that shows the requirements themselves unsuitable
(answered by cancelling and re-tendering with revised requirements)
[2][13]. Confidence is moderate: the primaries on amendment,
clarification and negotiation are an organisation's manual and two
public-sector practitioner guides, and no private-group procurement
guide could be opened; the vocabulary and the seam are the same in
the vendor and consultancy material that was opened. The
disconfirming query found the "living RFP" position, but as a
methodological argument for outcome-based, co-created procurement
[10][11], not as a description of how an RFP is run today; even
there a document is issued and final offers are made against it.
The unexpected find: the CIPS cycle, the procurement institutes'
own model, does not contain the word RFP at all [1].

## Question

Card 5: where the document ends and the process begins.

- Question: What of the RFP process (vendor questions and answers,
  amendments, security and compliance questionnaires, evaluation,
  shortlist, best-and-final offers, contract) belongs in the RFP
  document and its annexes, what returns upstream into the
  requirements (vendor answers as sources, changes of what is
  wanted), and where a framework for specifying should stop; against
  the alternative that the document cannot be separated from the
  process.
- Why it matters: The boundary the principal asked for: the
  framework specifies and does not run procurement; what comes back
  from vendors must have a place in the chain.
- What would answer it: Procurement process models (the sourcing
  cycle of the procurement institutes) and practitioner guidance on
  the RFP lifecycle.
- Where to look: procurement institutes' process models,
  sourcing-software vendors' process descriptions (vendor claims),
  practitioner guidance on vendor Q&A, amendments and
  clarifications; more than one route. Known items: a sourcing cycle
  with the RFP placed in it; guidance on handling vendor questions
  and amendments.
- Disconfirming query: Expected: a clean boundary between the
  document and the process. Search for the opposite: that the RFP is
  a living document amended through the process and cannot be
  frozen.
- Stop rule: Nothing new from a differently-worded query, and each
  perspective of the card (procurement officer, evaluator) has a
  primary source; or about fifteen openings spent.

The baseline the note must beat: the principal's own working rule
that the forge specifies and does not run procurement, with no
stated place for what vendors send back and no stated point at which
the specifying stops. A note that only restates "document here,
process there" adds nothing; it must say where the seam is, by whose
account, and what crosses it in each direction.

## Key findings

**F1. The institutes' and manuals' process models place the RFP as
one stage of a cycle: documentation before it, evaluation,
negotiation and award after it.** [consensus; evidence: an
institute's process model and an organisation's purchasing manual]
The CIPS Procurement and Supply Cycle runs thirteen stages:
Specification, Analysis, Strategy, Market, Documentation ("Prepare
tender documents, including a detailed specification and service
levels"), Criteria (RFI and due diligence to choose who takes part),
Tender ("Send an Invitation to Tender (ITT) and Request for
Quotation (RFQ) to selected suppliers"), Evaluation ("bid evaluation
and validation against pre-defined award criteria"; "Post-tender
negotiation often takes place, with multiple parties involved"),
Contract, then delivery and management stages [1]. The USPS
Supplying Principles and Practices run the same sequence as numbered
sections: 2-24 Review and Finalize RFP, 2-27 Issue RFPs, 2-29 Issue
Amendments, 2-30 Receive and Record Proposals, 2-31 Implement
Proposal Evaluation Strategy, 2-37 Hold Discussions, 2-38 Negotiate
with Suppliers, 2-40 Produce Contract Award Recommendation, 3-1
Award Contract, 3-3 Provide Feedback [3]. Both rest on a summarising
fetch of the page.

**F2. Before the proposal due date the RFP is a document under
change control: vendor questions are answered to all, and every
change of substance is an amendment that becomes part of the RFP.**
[consensus; evidence: an organisation's purchasing manual, a vendor's
process guide, corroborated by the many agency addenda the searches
returned but which were not opened] USPS 2-29: "RFPs must be amended
when changes such as quantity, specifications, delivery schedule"
are needed, the list including the "date of receipt of proposals"
and corrections of ambiguities or defects; "Once an amendment is
approved, it becomes an integral part of the RFP"; amendments "must
be provided to all suppliers that received a copy of the RFP";
"suppliers must be provided sufficient time to consider the
amendment and revise their proposals"; information released to
improve competition goes out "as an amendment to the RFP or as
general information provided for the purpose of clarification"; a
verbal change needs "confirmation by written amendment" [2]. The
same section says the team decides, by the complexity of the change,
whether to amend, to cancel and resolicit, or to take other action
[2]. The section covers the period between issue and receipt of
proposals only [2]. Responsive's guide puts the question channel
into the document: "Set up a way for vendors to ask questions if
they need clarification," with firm deadlines [9] (vendor claim). The
inference that the document is therefore versioned up to the due
date and not after it is the agent's own, drawn from where the
sources place the mechanism.

**F3. After the due date two separate exchanges run, each with
narrow rules, and neither writes into the RFP: clarification of what
was submitted, and negotiation with the leading bidder.**
[consensus; evidence: two public-sector practitioner guides and an
organisation's manual] Procurement Journey: contact with bidders is
"purely to clarify aspects of the tender which are unclear or
abnormal"; "Negotiations in relation to price or other areas where
bid improvements may be possible should not take place"; "all
communications with bidders must be properly recorded so that an
audit trail is maintained"; post-tender negotiation "should not
distort competition" and is a separate exercise [7]. HEPA: "PTN is
contact between you and the leading tenderer with a view to improving
or enhancing Value for Money (VFM)"; it "does not change the outcome
of the competition"; "must not become any form of 'Dutch Auction'";
"must be a controlled and documented process" and "should not be
confused with 'tender' or 'bid clarification'" [8]. USPS separates
discussions (2-37) from negotiations (2-38); "The goal of
negotiations is to come to final agreement on contract terms and
conditions, including contract type and price" [6][5]. These
sources are of public-sector origin; a private group is not bound by
them, but the opened vendor and consultancy material uses the same
stages and vocabulary [9][10].

**F4. The RFP's life ends by being frozen into the contract as an
exhibit, with the proposal below it and the agreement above both.**
[single source, tertiary; evidence: an aggregator of filed contract
clauses] A sample clause ranks the documents "1) the Agreement with
all exhibits, 2) the RFP, and 3) the Contractor's Proposal", with
the RFP defined to include "all documents, exhibits, attachments,
and addenda thereto" [14]. The agency contracts and templates the
searches returned, which would be the primaries, could not be opened
(binary or oversize PDFs), so this finding rests on the aggregator
alone. USPS gives the negotiation's end as "final agreement on
contract terms and conditions" without saying how the RFP enters the
contract [6].

**F5. Security and compliance questionnaires are instruments with
publishers of their own, referenced by an RFP, not written in it.**
[single source for the primary, vendor and secondary snippets beside
it; evidence: the publisher's own page] The Cloud Security Alliance
describes the CAIQ as "built into the CCM", offering "a simple set of
'yes/no' questions to assess cloud providers" that saves "repetitive
evaluations"; providers "self-assess and submit to the STAR
Registry"; version 4.1 of CCM and CAIQ are combined; the page does
not mention procurement or RFPs [12]. The Shared Assessments SIG page
was behind a sign-in interstitial and is not cited for content. The
search results, not opened, describe the questionnaire as a
due-diligence track run alongside the RFP rather than inside it.

**F6. The RFP document carries the rules of the process it will be
run under; the seam is not "requirements in the document, process
outside".** [consensus; evidence: an organisation's manual and a
vendor's process guide] USPS 2-24 requires that the "RFP describes
how proposals will be evaluated" and "states the Postal Service's
expectations regarding data rights and intellectual property" [4].
Responsive lists in the document the company background, goals,
specifications, timeline, budget constraints and "how responses will
be evaluated", beside the question channel and deadlines [9]. The
search results on single point of contact and uniform Q&A (not
opened) say the same.

**F7. What returns upstream is a changed requirement, by one of two
routes: amendment before the due date, cancellation and re-tender
after it.** [single source for the second route, consensus for the
first; evidence: a purchasing manual and one cancellation notice] The
amendment route is F2 [2]. The second route: a Danish gaming company
cancelled an IT-services tender because "the contracting authority
has realised that some of the requirements were unclear and
unsuitable", "giving room for mixed interpretations, and therefore
making it difficult to evaluate the proposals", and announced that
"The contract will be available in a new and revised tender shortly"
(notice published 11 April 2012, after a closing date of 13 December
2011) [13]. The notice is read on Mercell, a copy of the OJEU notice.
Search snippets named further cases (a South African payroll tender
reissued after no bid passed the mandatory requirements; an
Australian museum re-tendering on reduced scope) that were not
opened. No source was found that treats vendor answers or proposals
as requirement sources in the requirements-engineering sense (see Not
found).

**F8. The disconfirming position exists: the RFP as a short
outcome statement refined with vendors in working sessions, or as a
package that is "continuously" refined. It is argued as a better
method, not described as current practice, and even there a document
is issued and final offers are made against it.** [contested;
evidence: a consultancy opinion and a vendor claim, no measured
outcomes] An Accenture consultant proposes to "develop a short RFP
inviting vendors to co-create solutions with the agency", where
"vendors and agency staff would work side-by-side to openly exchange
information", "Assumptions would be jointly discussed and agreed
upon", and "final offers to capture the details of the mutually
developed solution" are submitted; statutory and rule-based terms
stay in the RFP as fixed [10]. A sourcing-software vendor contrasts
"the static nature of traditional RFPs" with a method that lets teams
"continuously refine their strategies and adapt to evolving
requirements", noting that "your business stakeholders are updating
their requirements, engineers are updating their designs", without
saying how the package is revised or versioned [11]. Neither source
says that the document cannot be frozen; both move the iteration
into the solution, which is co-developed, while the outcome statement
and the legal terms stay. Contradicted in emphasis by [2], [7] and
[8], which treat change after the due date as the exception that is
controlled, recorded and kept from distorting competition.

## Unexpected

The institutes' model has no RFP in it. The CIPS cycle names the RFI
(stage 6, "to gain insights into suppliers") and the ITT and RFQ
(stage 7) and never the RFP; the RFP is the vocabulary of the US and
of the software and advisory market [1][9]. A forge artefact or
render called "RFP" would name one member of a family (RFI, RFQ,
ITT, RFP) whose members differ by what the supplier is asked to
propose; the card did not ask which, and the note does not answer
it.

The purchasing manual gives the buyer a three-way choice when a
change is needed, by the size of the change: amend, cancel and
resolicit, or other action [2]. The card asked what returns upstream
as if it were one channel; the sources show the size of the change
deciding whether the document is patched or the round is restarted.

The agile route of [10] moves the solution design out of the
document into the dialogue with shortlisted vendors, with final
offers capturing "the mutually developed solution". That bears on the
other cards' premortem that for a COTS or SaaS purchase the solution
design in the RFP poisons the competition: here it is removed from
the document by design, for every archetype. A lead for cards 2 and
3, not a conclusion of this one.

## Options

A. The forge's RFP, whether artefact or render, ends at the proposal
due date. It is versioned by amendment up to that date: a vendor
question that shows a requirement unclear is answered by a change in
the chain and a new version of the RFP, the questions and answers
recorded. Everything after the date (clarification, discussions,
negotiation, final offers, the contract) is the procurement officer's
and lives outside the forge; proposals and clarifications come in as
sources only when the principal brings them. Trade-off: the forge
holds the one mechanism the sources show changing the document [2]
and leaves the equal-treatment process it has no business running
[7][8]; the cost is that the Q&A record, which the process treats as
part of the RFP, must have a home in the forge.

B. The forge ends before the RFP: the chain stops at the BRD or the
solution design, the RFP is a render with person-supplied sections,
and an amendment is a re-render after the upstream change. Trade-off:
fits the "change upstream, regenerate" discipline the sources'
amendment rule resembles (a change of specification is issued as an
amendment, never edited into the text [2]); but a render has no
history of its own in the forge, and the RFP's Q&A and amendment
record would then need a carrier of its own. Whether the RFP is an
artefact or a render is the question of the other cards; this
option is listed because the amendment mechanism bears on it.

C. The forge carries the process too: scoring, shortlist, final
offers. Trade-off: against the principal's bound, and the sources
show the process to be records, audit trail and equal-treatment
rules [7][8], not thinking to forge.

## Relevance to this project

The recommendation is option A: the seam at the proposal due date,
the document and its amendments on the forge's side, the exchanges
after it on the procurement officer's. It rests on F2 and F3, which
put the one mechanism that changes the document before that date and
the mechanisms that run after it under rules that forbid writing
into it, and on F6, which shows that the document must carry the
rules of the process (question channel, evaluation method, deadlines,
single point of contact) as a person-supplied section the chain does
not derive. It is chosen against B because the Q&A and amendment
record is treated by the process as part of the RFP [2] and needs a
versioned home, which a render does not give; and against C by the
principal's own bound and by the nature of what the process
produces.

What crosses the seam, in the agent's reading of the sources: inward,
vendor questions and later proposals and clarifications, which arrive
as sources (immutable, indexed, cited by provenance) exactly as the
forge already treats external input; upstream, a changed requirement,
through the intent first, then the layers, then a new version of the
RFP (amendment) or, after the due date, a new RFP for a new round
(cancel and re-tender, F7). The security and compliance
questionnaires are referenced instruments with their own publishers
(F5): the RFP names which ones and who must pass them, a person
supplies the gates, and the forge derives neither. The contract
freezes the RFP as an exhibit below the agreement (F4); the forge
should know this only to say that the version issued is the version
that binds, and keep it immutable from the due date on. These
inferences are the agent's own; the sources give the mechanisms, not
the mapping onto the forge.

The disconfirming finding (F8) does not move the recommendation but
sharpens one thing: where a project chooses the outcome-based route,
the solution design stays out of the issued document and the chain's
lower layers are inputs to the dialogue, not to the RFP. That is for
cards 2 and 3 to settle.

Consult when: deciding where the RFP artefact or render ends and what
its versions are; designing how vendor questions, answers and
amendments are recorded in a project; a vendor question or an
evaluation shows a requirement unclear or unsuitable and the round
must be patched or restarted; the principal asks to carry evaluation
scoring, shortlists or final offers in the forge; naming the artefact
(RFP against RFI, RFQ, ITT).

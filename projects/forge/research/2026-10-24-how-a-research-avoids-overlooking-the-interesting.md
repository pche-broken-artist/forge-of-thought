# How a research avoids overlooking the interesting and the unexpected

## Question

A research of the forge is one question, answered by Claude through
isolated web-searching subagents, soon from a plan the principal
confirms before any search runs. One of his four demands is that it
"does not overlook interesting things". What does the literature on
information seeking, on innovation and distant knowledge, on
structured contrarian analysis and on LLM research systems say about
how a search widens its net without filling it with noise: how the
unexpected gets a way in, what the plan and the agents' briefing must
carry for that, when a detour pays and when it is noise, and how a
research knows it has looked wide enough?

The baseline it must beat is the current command: a topic, a search
for "current best practices, established frameworks, and notable
recent developments", one note. Nothing in it names a mechanism for
the unexpected; whether the interesting is found depends on the
agent's disposition on the day. The companion note of the same date
covers source quality and the exclusion of junk; this note is the
opposite force and does not repeat it.

## Key findings

**F1. Real searches evolve; the query at the end is not the query at
the start, and most of the finding happens by techniques other than
the keyword query.** [consensus; 1] Bates's berrypicking model
(1989) holds that "the query itself (as well as the search terms
used) is continually shifting, in part or whole", that the result is
"a series of selections of individual references and bits of
information" rather than one result set, and that the classic
one-query model "represents some searches, but not all, perhaps not
even the majority". Her six techniques are footnote chasing
(backward from a found work), citation searching (forward to works
that cite it), the journal run, area scanning (browsing what sits
next to a find), subject searches, author searching. She ties the
unexpected to two of them: area scanning's jumps across related
areas produce "serendipitous discoveries", and citation groupings
are "likely to be stimulating to creativity" because citing works
may be unrelated by subject yet share a thread. Her design remark
"it is precisely what is not indexed that we want to sample" is the
oldest statement of the problem this note is about: the interesting
is by definition not where the query points.

**F2. Exploratory search is the mode in which the goal is learning
and investigation, not lookup; it combines querying with browsing,
and the field admits it has no metric for how well exploration is
supported.** [consensus on the concept; the evidence base is thin;
2, 3, 4] Marchionini (2006) separates lookup from learn and
investigate, and argues the latter two need browsing strategies
where lookup is served by analytic ones [2, via 3 and 4]. The
special-section introduction of the same issue says exploratory
searches "are often motivated by a complex information problem, and
a poor understanding of terminology and information space
structure", that users "combine querying and browsing strategies to
foster learning and investigation", and, plainly: "No metrics exist
to determine how well a system supports exploration" [3]. A 2023
review of 231 publications on exploratory search (2010 to 2021)
finds 77% evaluated systems rather than the search process, 75%
used students and academics as participants, and measurement was
inconsistent [4]. So the concept is settled; the evidence on what
makes exploration succeed is weak, and what follows below leans on
qualitative studies and on adjacent fields.

**F3. Serendipity is not luck: it arises from conditions and
strategies, it happens during the search for something else, and
people who experience it often have named what they do.**
[consensus across small qualitative studies; 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]
Foster and Ford (2003), interviewing interdisciplinary scholars,
reinterpret serendipity as "a phenomenon arising from both
conditions and strategies", purposive and non-purposive at once
[5]; valuable unexpected information came either from looking in
"likely" sources or "by chance", and a find either reinforced the
researcher's conception of the task or reconfigured it [6, 5 via
6]. Erdelez defines information encountering as the "unexpected
discovery of useful or interesting information ... during the
search for some other information", notes that it also covers
"unforeseen characteristics of information that had been sought",
and found that some people, "super-encounterers", encounter far
more than others and treat it as a way of acquiring information [6,
7]. Makri and Blandford's process model (28 interdisciplinary
researchers, critical-incident interviews) has three elements that
every later study keeps: an unexpected circumstance, an insightful
connection, a valuable outcome [8]; McCay-Peet and Toms (12
professionals) consolidate the same into trigger, connection,
follow-up, valuable outcome, with an "unexpected thread" running
through [10]. The strategies study (14 creative professionals who
"frequently experience serendipity") found all of them adopting
strategies "to support stages of the serendipity process", and
names seven: "varying their routines, being observant, making
mental space, relaxing their boundaries, drawing on previous
experiences, looking for patterns and seizing opportunities"; the
authors add that academic researchers in their earlier study
described the same strategies "albeit using varied terminology"
[9]. For a mechanical research the translation is direct: vary the
route (other techniques than the query, F1), relax the boundary
(adjacent fields, F6), look for patterns across what the agents
bring back, and give the unexpected a place to be seized rather
than brushed aside.

**F4. The unexpected is lost by default: it is "brushed aside", and
the willingness to change direction varies with the person and
falls under stress and time pressure.** [emerging; 6] McBirnie
(2008, via Erdelez and Makri) found people occasionally stored
unexpected information for later but "more often the information
was 'brushed aside and lost'"; some participants were "much less
willing to 'change direction' than others"; stress and anxiety
lower that willingness, extroversion raises it, and time pressure
is a contextual factor [6]. Kuhlthau's information search process
places the rise of uncertainty and confusion in the exploration
stage, "the dip" of confidence [12, secondary]. For a research run
by agents with a brief and a budget, the lesson is that the
unexpected needs a mandated place to land (a section the agent must
fill or declare empty) and an explicit permission to let a find
reconfigure the question, because a brief that only says "answer
this" will have the agent discard what does not answer it.

**F5. The highest-impact work rests on a conventional mass with an
injection of the atypical; novel combinations are rare, and novelty
without the conventional base does worse than either.** [consensus;
large-N, replicated across 243 fields and five decades; 21] Uzzi,
Mukherjee, Stringer and Jones (Science, 2013; 17.9 million papers)
find science "typically relies on highly conventional combinations
and rarely incorporates journal pairs that are uncommon compared to
chance"; papers that combine "high median conventionality and high
tail novelty are hits in 9.11 out of 100 papers, a rate nearly
double the background rate of 5%"; papers high on one dimension
only "have hit rates about half as large"; papers low on both, 2.05
per 100. Teams insert tail novelty 37.7% more often than solo
authors [21]. For a research this fixes the proportion: the plan's
body stays conventional (the question's own field, its primary
sources), and the unexpected is a deliberate minority of the
search, not its mode; a research that is all detour does worse
than one that is all focus.

**F6. Distant knowledge pays, and it pays most in the upper tail of
novelty; the people most likely to see it are marginal to the
field, and brokers between fields carry it.** [consensus in
innovation research; effects modest but consistent; 16, 17, 20,
22, 24, 25] Jeppesen and Lakhani (166 broadcast problems, 12,000
scientists, InnoCentive): "for every increment increase in the
self-assessed 'Problem-Expertise Distance' scale, the odds of
creating a winning solution increase by 3.3%" (10% level);
marginality works because the marginal "are not bound to the
current thinking in the field of the focal problem and therefore
can offer perspectives and heuristics that are novel" [20]. Franke,
Poetz and Schreier (Management Science, 2014): solvers from
analogous markets produce ideas of "lower potential for immediate
use" but "substantially higher levels of novelty", the effect
"amplified when the analogous distance between the markets
increases" and "particularly strong in the upper tail of the
novelty distribution" [22]. Rosenkopf and Nerkar (optical disks):
exploration that stays inside the firm has "consistently lower
impact"; impact beyond the domain is greatest when exploration
crosses both the organisational and the technological boundary
[17]. Hargadon and Sutton's IDEO ethnography: a firm working in
over 40 industries innovates by introducing "known solutions where
they are unknown" [25]; Burt: between-group brokers "are more
likely to express ideas, less likely to have ideas dismissed, and
more likely to have ideas evaluated as valuable" [24]. Fleming
(2001) gives the price: "experimentation with new components and
new combinations leads to less useful inventions on average, but
it also implies an increase in the variability that can result in
both failure and breakthrough" [16]. The research's adjacent-field
probe should therefore be read as a variance-raising bet: expect
most of it to be unusable, and expect the one find that matters to
come from it.

**F7. Experts penalise distance and novelty: the closer an
evaluator is to a proposal's field, the lower the score, and highly
novel proposals are scored lowest of all.** [emerging; one
randomised study; 23] Boudreau, Guinan, Lakhani and Riedl (142
researchers, 150 proposals, 2,130 triple-blinded evaluator-proposal
pairs at a medical school): "evaluators systematically give lower
scores to research proposals that are closer to their own areas of
expertise and to those that are highly novel"; the novelty penalty
is "proposals with particularly high levels of novelty" (at low
novelty, scores rise with novelty); the authors' reading is bounded
rationality, "lesser experts simply cannot 'see' what experts can
see" and experts misjudge what lies outside their paradigm; "no
amount of aggregation and averaging, blinding, or other
conventional policies can address this problem" [23]. For the forge
this is a warning about the synthesis step: an "expert" lens, human
or model, applied to the agents' returns will tend to discard the
very finds the exploration was for. The unexpected must be shown to
the principal before it is judged, not after.

**F8. The trade-off is an inverted U: searching wider and deeper
helps up to a point and then hurts; the fall comes from diminishing
returns and from unreliability at a distance; and most searchers
stop long before the top.** [consensus on the shape; 18, 19] Laursen
and Salter (U.K. manufacturing): "searching widely and deeply is
curvilinearly (taking an inverted U-shape) related to performance";
Hecker (2016) confirms it on 49,919 firms in 13 countries, Garriga
et al. (2013) on Swiss firms [19]. Katila and Ahuja (robotics)
found the inverted U for depth (reusing the same knowledge) but
only the rising, linear part for scope (new knowledge); their
explanation is that "only few companies 'over-search' in this
dimension, due to high costs of this search approach", in line with
"the human tendency to try to reduce uncertainty" when choosing
between "known, well-tried directions vs. searching in uncertain,
new directions" [18]. Two consequences for a research: the risk of
too much scope is real but, on the evidence, rarely reached, while
the risk of too much depth (another source on the same point) is
the common one; and the mechanism of the fall is named - a find far
from the question is harder to judge correctly (Heiner's
reliability argument, via 18), which is F7 again from the other
side.

**F9. LLM search agents over-search, stagnate and repeat; the
measured remedies are a novelty signal (new documents as a share of
retrieved), a coverage signal (criteria satisfied) and a controller
that continues, intervenes or stops.** [emerging; 2026 studies; 37,
38] Xie et al. (Apple and Duke, EACL 2026) measure an over-search
rate of 70.5% (0.620 searches per query against an optimal 0.364);
noisy retrieval raised cost per correct answer 3.6 times without
better abstention; a deep-research configuration cost 221 times the
base; raising reasoning effort lifted accuracy 74.1 to 74.6 while
cost almost tripled; the one strong lever was negative evidence
(abstention 100% when only negative documents were retrieved, 0%
when only positive), yet negative documents were only 13 to 22% of
what retrieval returned [38]. Soudani et al. (2026) find "the
majority of iterations contribute little or no improvement" in deep
research agents, which "lack awareness of their own trajectories";
their controller computes search novelty as "the proportion of
retrieved documents D_t that do not appear in D_{1:t-1}" and
coverage as the average of per-criterion weights (0, 0.5, 1), and
chooses continue, intervene (a "critical re-thinker" proposes a new
query) or stop when "the trajectory is exhausted"; on
BrowseComp-Plus it cut search calls by about 14 on average while
raising recall and accuracy (Tongyi-DR recall 48.4 to 59.0, calls
77.8 to 33.6) [37]. Two things carry over: the unexpected needs a
counter-force to the agent's default of repeating itself, and the
stopping rule can be stated in kind (no new documents, every
criterion touched) rather than in a count.

**F10. The one published account of a production multi-agent
research system teaches breadth first, effort scaled to the
question, and parallel agents as the cure for path dependence.**
[single source, vendor's own; 36] Anthropic (2025): agents should
"start with short, broad queries, evaluate what's available, then
progressively narrow focus", because left alone they "write overly
long, specific queries that return few results"; explicit scaling
rules ("1 agent with 3-10 tool calls" for a fact, "2-4 subagents
with 10-15 calls each" for a comparison, "more than 10 subagents
with clearly divided responsibilities" for complex research) were
needed to stop "spawning 50 subagents for simple queries"; "each
subagent needs an objective, an output format, guidance on the
tools and sources to use, and clear task boundaries", vague briefs
causing duplicated work; parallel subagents with their own context
"reduce path dependency and enable thorough, independent
investigations"; the system "outperformed single-agent Claude Opus
4 by 90.2%" on an internal evaluation at "about 15x more tokens
than chats", and token usage alone explained 80% of performance
variance on BrowseComp [36]. The forge already runs one agent per
question; what it does not yet do is give any agent a divergent
brief.

**F11. The field has eight ways to decide when searching is enough,
none of them evaluated; the foraging rule is "leave the patch when
its rate of return falls below the average", and saturation is
confirmed in practice by a re-run that changes nothing.** [consensus
on the catalogue; little evaluation; 11, 13, 14] Booth (2010)
identifies eight methods: capture-recapture, feedback from the
commissioner, seeking the disconfirming case, comparison against a
known gold standard, retrieval of known items, the law of
diminishing returns, a priori stopping rules, theoretical
saturation, and concludes "there has been very little formal
evaluation of the specific strengths and weaknesses of the
different techniques" [13]. Pirolli and Card's patch model applies
Charnov's marginal value theorem: leave when the current patch's
instantaneous rate falls below the environment's mean rate; Wilke
et al. note that for humans "we have found scarcely any tests" [11,
secondary]. Rohwer et al. (2021) re-ran a qualitative evidence
synthesis and found the update "confirmed and sometimes enriched
evidence within themes but made little or no substantive
difference to the theory and overall findings", which they call
thematic saturation [14]. Of Booth's eight, three fit a research
of the forge without a gold standard: known items (did the agents
find what the principal or Claude already knew should be there),
the disconfirming case (was a dissenting source sought and found or
found absent), and diminishing returns stated as F9's novelty
signal.

**F12. The intelligence community's structured techniques put the
assumption check and the outside-in sweep at the start of a
project, treat the "unlikely" with two dedicated methods, require
an outsider in every brainstorm, and reserve scenario work for
"unknown unknowns".** [practitioner consensus; the efficacy
evidence is thin, F13; 27, 28, 29] The CIA primer (2009) says
analysts "must actively review the accuracy of their mind-sets by
applying structured analytic techniques that will make those mental
models more explicit and expose their key assumptions". The Key
Assumptions Check "is most useful at the beginning of an analytic
project", takes "an hour or two", and its goal "is not to undermine
or abandon key assumptions; rather, it is to make them explicit and
identify what information or developments would demand rethinking
them". Outside-In Thinking is "most useful at the conceptualization
of an analytic project, when the goal is to identify all the
critical, external factors", and "can reduce the risk of missing
important variables early in the analytic process"; it begins by
listing the external forces (social, technological, economic,
environmental, political) over which one has no influence, then
asks whether each actually bears on the issue. High-Impact/
Low-Probability "sensitizes analysts to the potential impact of
seemingly low probability events" and "can alert analysts to
oversights in the mainstream analytic line"; "What If?" "assumes
that an event has occurred ... and explains how it might come
about", shifting "from whether an event could occur to how it may
happen", and is "particularly important when a judgment rests on
limited information or unproven assumptions". Brainstorming must
"involve at least one 'outsider' in the process - that is, someone
who does not share the same educational background, culture,
technical knowledge or mind-set as the core group but has some
familiarity with the topic", and "never censor an analyst's ideas
no matter how unconventional". Alternative Futures is "extremely
useful in highly ambiguous situations, when analysts confront not
only a lot of 'known unknowns' but also 'unknown unknowns'" [27].
Coulthart's systematic review (2015) found "moderate to strong
evidence" for three techniques only - Analysis of Competing
Hypotheses, Brainstorming, Devil's Advocacy - and that one analyst
in three never uses any [29]; the Key Assumptions Check and
Outside-In Thinking are not among the evaluated three. For the
plan the principal confirms, three of these translate word for
word: the plan's assumptions listed and shown (what the research
takes for granted about where the answer lives), an outside-in
line (which fields outside the question's own could bear on it,
and which the plan deliberately leaves out), and the outsider rule
as one agent whose brief is another field.

**F13. Assigned opposition is weaker than real dissent, but
structured conflict still beats consensus; "steelmanning" has a
definition and no evidence.** [contested; 30, 31, 33] Nemeth (2001)
found authentic dissent produced more original thoughts and more
consideration of the opposing position, while a role-played devil's
advocate "mostly bolstered the initial view"; her later work holds
that the technique "does not stimulate divergent thinking or the
quality of decisions and solutions as authentic dissent does" [30].
Against that, Schweiger, Sandberg and Rechner (1989) found both
devil's advocacy and dialectical inquiry gave "significantly higher
quality decisions compared to consensus groups", with no difference
between the two; Schwenk's 1989 meta-analysis re-read the same
studies and reached conclusions differing from an earlier review;
the operationalisations were disputed [31]. Steelmanning, "the
strongest possible version of someone else's argument", is a
rationalist-community term of unclear origin with no controlled
study found; a community critique calls it "an especially
insidious form of strawmanning" when it replaces the other side's
actual argument with one's own improvement of it [33]. The lesson
for a research: a contrarian agent asked to "argue against" from
the same sources is the role-played advocate; a contrarian agent
asked to find real dissenters (named people, named papers, named
failures) is closer to authentic dissent, and that is the
difference between a brief that says "disagree" and one that says
"find who disagrees and why".

**F14. The premortem works by prospective hindsight: assuming the
outcome has happened produces more and more specific reasons than
asking what might go wrong; the often-quoted 30% is secondhand.**
[emerging; the primary numbers not verified here; 32] Klein's HBR
piece (2007) rests on Mitchell, Russo and Pennington (1989), which
compared explanations for outcomes framed as certain versus merely
possible; the "about 30%" improvement in identifying reasons is
repeated by every secondary source and was not confirmed against
the 1989 paper here. Veinott, Klein and Wiggins (ISCRAM 2010; 178
students, five conditions) report that imagining the plan had
already failed reduced confidence about twice as much as pros/cons
or cons-only; a 2018 poster by Veinott found premortem groups
generated more non-redundant reasons for failure than worst-case
groups, but not more solutions [32]. Applied to a research plan the
form is: "the research is finished and the principal says it missed
the most interesting thing; what was it?" The answer to that is a
probe the plan can carry.

**F15. The one published mechanism that measurably widens coverage
in LLM research is perspective-guided questioning; the one that
measurably raises perceived serendipity is a moderator that
re-asks from retrieved information nobody used.** [emerging; one
lab, two papers; 34, 35] STORM (Shao et al., NAACL 2024) discovers
perspectives by asking for related Wikipedia topics, fetching their
tables of contents and asking the model for five perspectives that
together cover the topic, always adding a "basic fact writer"; each
perspective asks one question per turn for up to five turns, the
answers grounded in filtered search. Ablation: removing the
simulated conversation drops heading soft recall 86.26 to 77.97
(GPT-3.5) and unique references collected 99.83 to 39.56; removing
perspectives drops references to 54.36. Editors preferred it on
organisation (+25% absolute) and coverage (+10%), but gave lower
verifiability in over half the cases where they preferred the
baseline, attributing it to "red herring fallacy or
overspeculation", unverifiable links between facts; seven of ten
editors found the articles "emotional" or "unneutral" because
source bias transfers; inter-annotator agreement was low (alpha
0.22 to 0.39) [34]. Co-STORM (Jiang et al., 2024) adds a moderator
that "generates informed questions based on the uncited sources
retrieved since the last moderator turn", reranked by relevance to
the topic and dissimilarity to the question that retrieved them,
which "prioritizes information that does not directly answer the
original question but is relevant to the topic"; the moderator
intervenes when two consecutive expert turns only answer; the
human study (n=19) rated serendipity 3.90 against 2.70 for a search
engine (70% win, p=0.030) and judged 89% of moderator utterances as
"steering the discourse towards a new and interesting direction";
the authors do not measure unknown unknowns directly, only
perceived serendipity [35]. Both mechanisms are cheap to imitate in
a plan: name the perspectives before the questions, and give one
agent the leftovers.

**F16. Models are less epistemically diverse than a web search,
larger models less than smaller, and people assisted by one model
converge; retrieval helps.** [emerging; 39, 40] Wright et al.
(2025; 27 models, 155 topics, 12 countries): "every system is less
diverse than a search baseline", "large models are
counterintuitively less diverse than smaller ones", and "RAG can
improve diversity" [39]. Doshi and Hauser (Science Advances, 2024;
500 writers): access to generative ideas made individual stories
rated more creative, but "generative AI-enabled stories are more
similar to each other than stories by humans alone", a "social
dilemma" where the individual gains and the collective narrows
[40]. For the forge this is the reason the companion note of
2026-10-23 gave for other engines (shared priors, shared blind
spots): a plan whose perspectives are all generated by one model
will be narrower than the web it searches, and the perspectives
the principal adds by hand are not a courtesy but the widest part
of the net.

**F17. Query diversification is practised and reported as gains,
never isolated as recall.** [single sources; 41] Agentic AutoSurvey
generates "20-30 search variations to comprehensively capture
relevant literature"; SPAR uses query decomposition and "query
evolution" and reports up to +56% F1 over the best baseline; an RL
agent proposes several query variants searched at once [41]. No
study was found that measures recall as a function of query
diversity for literature search; the surveys of deep research
(2025) list diversification among methods and "novelty and broad
coverage" among metrics without settling their effect [42]. The
practice is sound by F1 and F10; the number of variants is not
evidence-based.

**F18. No controlled study was found of the prompt "what would an
expert be surprised I missed", or of "what am I missing"; the
nearest work is a metacognitive re-asking and a critic agent, and
LLM judgement of novelty itself has known limits.** [gap; 44] Hills
(2025) proposes a "Could you be wrong?" prompt that draws out
"errors, biases, contradictory evidence, and alternatives, none of
which were apparent in their initial response", as a demonstration
without controls; MetaCrit (2025/2026) adds monitoring and control
agents that critique soundness, tested on truthfulness not
coverage; a 2026 paper argues LLM-as-judge for scientific novelty
has real limits [44]. The question is therefore not evidence-backed
as a prompt; it is justified instead by F12 (assumption check and
premortem are the same question in two forms) and should be asked
of the principal and of a differently-briefed agent, not of the
agent that did the search (F7, F16).

**F19. The benchmarks of deep research measure comprehensiveness
and citation accuracy; none measures whether competing views or
contested claims are represented.** [gap; 43] DeepResearch Bench
(ICLR 2026) scores comprehensiveness and citation accuracy;
ReportBench finds "substantial room for improvement in terms of the
breadth and depth of research coverage"; LiveResearchBench scores
coverage and depth; none scores balance or the handling of dispute
[43]. The forge's own criterion for "contested" must therefore be
its own: a research that reports a position without saying who
disputes it, or that no one was found to, has not looked for the
contrary.

## Options

**A. The plan carries a Divergence section of fixed kinds, each
kind one agent with its own brief.** Beside the agreed questions,
the plan names three probes: an adjacent field (which other
discipline has solved a problem of this shape; brief: find the
analogue, not the answer), a dissent (who disagrees with the
expected answer and on what ground; brief: named people and
papers, or a statement that none was found after naming where it
was looked for), and what is new or contested (what changed in the
last two years, what the field itself calls open). Each probe
returns a separate "Unexpected" section with provenance, never
merged into the answer. Pro: the unexpected has a way in by
construction (F3, F12), the kinds are the ones with evidence (F6,
F13), the body of the research stays conventional (F5), and the
cost is bounded. Con: three more agents per research; the probes
are only as good as the field named, so naming it is the
principal's work in the plan.

**B. Perspective discovery before questions.** Before the
questions are drafted, Claude lists the perspectives from which the
topic is written about (STORM's move: what do neighbouring
treatments of the topic contain), shows them, and the principal
strikes and adds; each question is then tagged with the
perspectives it serves and the plan shows which perspective no
question serves. Pro: the strongest measured coverage gain (F15);
makes the plan's blind spots visible as empty rows; the principal's
additions are the widest part of the net (F16). Con: one more
round before searching; perspectives generated by the model are
narrower than they look (F16), so the step helps only if the
principal actually edits them.

**C. A leftovers pass after the agents return.** One agent reads
what the question agents cited or retrieved but did not use, and
the sources' own "see also" and reference lists (F1's footnote
chasing and area scanning), and returns "what this suggests the
research did not ask". Pro: the one mechanism that measurably
raised perceived serendipity (F15), Bates's techniques in agent
form, costs one agent. Con: needs the agents to return what they
discarded, which is a change to the agent's output shape; risks the
"red herring" failure of STORM (unverifiable links) unless the pass
is told to report leads, not conclusions.

**D. Red-team the plan, not the literature.** Before searching,
the plan itself gets a key assumptions check and a premortem: what
the plan assumes about where the answer lives, and "the research is
done and missed the most interesting thing; what was it?", the
answers shown to the principal with the plan. Pro: the two
techniques fit a plan exactly (F12, F14), run in minutes, and give
the principal the one thing he asked for before any token is spent;
also the only place for F18's question that F7 does not undermine.
Con: an assumption check produces assumptions, not finds; it tells
the plan where to look, it does not look.

**E. Trust the agents' curiosity ("note anything interesting").**
Pro: free. Con: no mechanism; the unexpected is brushed aside by
default (F4), agents repeat themselves (F9), and one model's
curiosity is narrower than the web (F16). Rejected; this is the
baseline.

**F. A novelty-and-coverage stopping rule in the agent's brief.**
The agent stops when a diversified query returns nothing it has
not seen and every perspective of its brief has at least one
primary source; it reports both facts. Pro: the measured cure for
stagnation and over-search (F9), stated in kind not count, gives
the research a way to say "wide enough" (F11). Con: a rule inside
an agent is observed only through its report; the forge cannot
verify it without logging the queries.

## Relevance to this project

Recommendation: **D, then B, then A, with C and F as the agents'
brief** - all five are cheap, they address different moments, and
none substitutes for another. In the order of the run:

**What the plan carries.** (1) The question and the conventional
body: the field, the kinds of primary source expected, the known
items the search must find (F11; if Claude or the principal knows
of one source that must appear, it is written down, and its absence
later is a finding about the search). (2) A Perspectives list (B):
from which standpoints the topic is treated, each question tagged
with the perspectives it serves, an empty perspective shown as
empty; the principal edits this list before anything else. (3) An
Assumptions line (D): what the plan takes for granted about where
the answer lives and what would make it rethink; and the premortem
sentence answered once by Claude, marked as his own. (4) A
Divergence section (A) of three probes, adjacent field, dissent,
new-or-contested, each one agent with a brief of its own; the
adjacent field named in the plan, not left to the agent, because
the naming is where the width is decided (F6, F12's outsider rule).
(5) A line on what is deliberately left out (outside-in: the
external fields considered and not pursued), so that "did not look"
is a statement and not a silence. The plan says these in kind; it
carries no counts beyond the number of agents the principal
confirms.

**What the agents' briefing says.** Start broad and narrow (F10):
short queries first, then the specific. Use more than the query
(F1): follow the references of a good source backward and its
citers forward, and scan what sits beside it. Keep two sections
apart in the return: the answer to the question, and "Unexpected"
with provenance, where anything that does not answer the question
but seems to matter is placed rather than dropped (F4), as a lead,
never as a conclusion (F15's red herring). A find may reconfigure
the question (F3): the agent says so in plain words ("the question
assumes X; source Y says X does not hold") and still answers the
question as put. Return the discards (C): the sources opened and
not used, one line each, so the leftovers pass has something to
read. Stop (F): when a differently-worded query brings nothing not
already seen and every perspective of the brief has a primary
source, and say which of the two ended the run. The dissent agent's
brief says "find who disagrees and on what ground", never "argue
against" (F13).

**How width is judged enough.** Not by a count. A research has
looked wide enough when the plan's perspectives each reached a
primary source or are reported empty with where it was looked; the
known items were found or their absence explained; the dissent
probe returned a dissenter or a statement of absence with the
places searched; the adjacent-field probe returned an analogue or
none; and the leftovers pass, reading what was opened and not used,
raised no lead the principal wants followed. A research that
satisfies these and still missed something has a plan defect, not a
search defect, and the plan is what the next round changes.

**When a detour pays and when it is noise.** The evidence gives
one test: a detour paid when it reconfigured the question or
supplied a mechanism from elsewhere (F3's reconfiguration, F6's
analogue), and it is noise when it adds a source on a point already
made (F8's depth is where over-search actually happens). The
synthesis therefore sorts the Unexpected sections by that test and
shows them to the principal before judging them (F7: the expert
lens discards exactly these), with Claude's view last and apart.
The body of the research stays conventional and the unexpected
stays a marked minority (F5); a research that is mostly detour is
the known failure mode, not the ambition.

**Consult when:** a research plan is drafted and the question is
which probes it carries; an agent's brief is written; a research
came back thin or all-confirming and the question is where the
dissent should have been; the principal asks "why did we not find
X" after the fact; a stopping rule is debated; the forge considers
a leftovers or novelty pass; a check of the research skill asks
whether "interesting things" has a mechanism or only a wish.
